III. Problem Background
Traditional PCB schematic design relies heavily on manual drawing and organizing,
resulting in cumbersome processes, long iteration cycles, and high rates of human
error. Currently, a large amount of hardware open-source materials, device drawings,
and chip reference designs are stored in static image formats such as PDF and PNG,
which cannot be directly edited, reused, or imported into projects, greatly limiting the
implementation of digitalization and automation of circuit design.
In recent years, intelligent schematic parsing empowered by artificial intelligence has
become an important research direction in the field of PCB- design EDA. The overall
solutions mainly fall into three technical categories:
First, conventional computer- vision- based solutions. Leveraging algorithms such as
edge detection, Hough transform, skeleton extraction, morphological processing and
connected- component analysis, these approaches perform basic segmentation and
extraction of wires, component contours and nodes. Template matching is further
adopted to recognize common standard components.
Second, deep- learning- based detection and segmentation solutions. Adopting
mainstream computer vision models including YOLO, Faster R- CNN, Mask R- CNN
and U- Net, these methods accomplish component object detection, pin- point

--- PAGE 2 ---
2
segmentation and semantic segmentation for wire regions. Combined with text
recognition algorithms such as CRNN and EasyOCR, the scheme extracts reference
designators, parameters and pin texts. Distance matching, IoU matching and spatial
orientation constraints are utilized to associate and bind components with texts, which
effectively improves the recognition robustness for complex schematic drawings.
Third, multimodal large language model fusion solutions. Relying on the general
perception capability of vision large models and image- text understanding models,
and combined with EDA domain- specific prior knowledge, this enables intelligent
understanding of non- standard components, blurry drawings, complex topologies and
ambiguous annotations. It mitigates the disadvantage of poor generalization and rigid
rules in traditional CV algorithms, and improves circuit parsing accuracy under
complex real- world scenarios.
Substantial efforts have been made and breakthroughs have been achieved in both
industry and academia for basic circuit parsing. Nevertheless, significant limitations
and numerous challenges still remain in the recognition and parsing of complex PCB
schematic diagrams. The technical difficulties are summarized as follows.
First, insufficient recognition accuracy for densely- packed components. Existing
models suffer large recognition errors under scenarios of high- density layout,
component overlap, partial occlusion and tiny surface- mount devices. They are prone
to missed detection, false detection and category confusion, and can hardly meet the
accuracy requirements for engineering- grade applications.
Second, low accuracy in pin localization and association. Conventional solutions can
only recognize the overall bounding boxes of components, and fail to precisely
distinguish pin coordinates and the correspondence between pin numbers for
multi- pin components. Common issues include pin misalignment, missed pin
recognition, and incorrect binding between pins and their corresponding components.
Third, poor fault tolerance for text- component association. Conventional IoU- based

--- PAGE 3 ---
3
matching and nearest- neighbor matching algorithms are prone to mismatching under
scenarios such as cluttered texts, tilted annotations and cross- component text
interference. As a result, precise semantic binding cannot be achieved between
reference designators, parameters, pin names and their corresponding components.
Fourth, weak capability for electrical parsing of complex topologies. Existing
algorithms struggle to accurately distinguish physical crossings and electrical
connections of wires. They produce large errors in connectivity judgment for
T- junctions, cross junctions, multi- branch traces and drawings with broken- wire
distortions, which readily leads to false connections, missing connections and
spurious nets.
To address the aforementioned technical difficulties and challenges, this competition
problem focuses on the recognition and automatic parsing of complex PCB schematic
diagrams, providing an innovation platform for tackling these bottlenecks. Figure 1
illustrates the characteristics of the problems to be solved in this competition task via
a representative example, which mainly includes the following two aspects.
1)The circuits to be parsed in this competition task feature large- scale size, a large
number of components, dense multi- pin devices, intricate connection relationships,
cluttered and interference- prone texts, as well as great difficulty in flying nets
suppression.
2)This competition task requires comprehensive information recognition, including
components (component key, type, name, value, and bbox), pins of each component
(pin number, pin name, and pin coordinates), as well as circuit connectivity (the set of
pins connected by each net and the corresponding segment collection of the net). In
addition, text recognition must be accomplished to fulfil the task accurately, which
poses substantial difficulties for complex PCB scenarios.

IV. Problem Description
i.Competition task
Given an image of a PCB circuit schematic, the task of this competition requires
participating teams to recognize structured schematic information and output the
results in the specified JSON format.
1.Input：Circuit schematic image. The dataset provided by the competition is in PNG
format. Participating teams may convert it into other formats for further processing
according to their needs, and no constraints are imposed by the competition on such
conversions.

a)‘components’： Instances of components in Figure1，For example `U1`、`R3`、
`C12`，including category, text information and bbox.
b)‘pins’ ： Pins of each component, including pin number, pin name and pin
coordinates.

B.ComponentF1
Components are matched directly by key. For example, U3 from Ground- Truth (GT)
is only compared against U3 from Prediction (Pred). A component is regarded as
correct only if all of the following conditions are satisfied simultaneously：
a)Key match；
b)Type correct；
c)The bbox coordinate error shall be within the threshold, with a default threshold of
‘20 px’.
Where text comparison is required, the text shall be an exact match.
Text comparison rules：
a)‘ic’：compare ‘Name’；

--- PAGE 10 ---
10
b)‘resistor’、‘capacitor’：compare ‘value’；
c)Other categories: No text comparison is performed.
C. PinF1
Pins are first matched by component key, then by pin number. A pin shall satisfy：
a)Pin name correct；
b)Pin number correct；
c)The pin coordinate distance shall be within the threshold, with a default threshold of
‘5 px’. For pin coordinates and pin connection modes, refer to the detailed description
in Appendix III.
D.NetHypergraphF1
Each net is treated as a pin set. During evaluation, Hungarian matching is performed
between Pred nets and GT nets, followed by calculating whether the component pins
within each net appear correctly.
E.NetLineF1
Lines are compared only within matched net pairs. Each edge_i serves as one segment
detection target. A Pred segment is regarded as correctly detected if both- endpoint
distances between the Pred segment and the GT segment are sufficiently close, with a
default threshold of ‘5 px’.
F.Notes
a)The component key is critical. Incorrect keys will affect the scoring of components,
pins and nets.
b)Strings adopt exact- match comparison. 10k, 10K and 10 k will be treated as distinct
strings.
c)The net key itself does not need to match the GT, but the pin- connection
relationships inside the hyperGraph shall be correct.

VII. Appendix
Appendix I：Detailed explanation of output JSON format
This case illustration takes the parsing result of the schematic diagram of the
Raspberry Pi RP2350 Pico2 development board (Figure 1) as an example to elaborate
on the output JSON. It covers format requirements, field definitions, typical examples,
and screenshot comparisons for the three sections: components, pins, and nets within
the target JSON.
1. Data Description
The output target JSON must contain three top- level fields.
a)component: Keyed by component designator, describing each component’s type,
name, value, and bounding- box.
b)pins: Keyed by component designator, with subordinate pin_* entries that describe
pin names and connection- point coordinates.
c)nets: Keyed by net name, describing the hyperGraph (logical connection
relationships) and edges (physical wire segments).
2. Coordinate system description
The coordinate system follows the lower- left origin of the original ParsedJson: the
x- axis increases to the right, and the y- axis increases upward. The bbox format is [x1,
y1, x2, y2], where (x1, y1) denotes the lower- left coordinate and (x2, y2) denotes the

upper- right coordinate.

8. JSON format example for HyperGraph (with flying nets)
What are flying nets?

--- PAGE 24 ---
24
Flying nets refer to multiple non- VCC/GND test points or net- label components with
identical names scattered at different physical locations on the schematic. They are
logically connected together by sharing the same net name. Other component pins
directly connected to these identically- named components belong to the same net
even if no physical wires are drawn between them.
In this case, RUN_1, RUN_2, RUN_3, and RUN_4 are four test- point / net- label
components with the same name, located at different positions on the schematic.
Together they form the RUN net:
 RUN_1 is directly connected to U3.26;
 RUN_2 is directly connected to U5.17;
 RUN_3 is directly connected to R8.1;
 RUN_4 is directly connected to SW2.1;
Although no wires are drawn between U3, U5, R8 and SW2 on the schematic, they
are logically part of the same net because all of them connect to the identically- named
RUN net. This is the flying- net relationship.

Summary of flying- net characteristics
a)Dispersed components with identical names: The core of a net with flying nets
consists of multiple identically- named net- labels or test points, such as RUN_1,
RUN_2, RUN_3, RUN_4.
b)Logical- only connection instead of physical connection: These identically- named
components have no connecting wires on the schematic but share the same net name.
c)Point- like segments for edges: Since flying nets have no physical wires, each

This description takes the schematic in Figure I as an example to illustrate the
judgment and representation methods for two common pin connection modes in the
target JSON.
a)Red pin connected by green wire: The wire endpoint coincides with the component
pin position, and the intersection point between the green wire and the red pin serves
as the pin coordinate.

x- axis increases to the right, and the y- axis increases upward. The format of a point is
{ "x": x, "y": y }.

Appendix IV: Common Notes
1. The component key is critical. Writing U3 as U8 will cause the component, its pins,
and associated nets to receive no score.
2. The pin key shall consistently adopt the format pin_<pin_number>, since pin
matching directly utilizes the pin number.
3. Pin entries in hyperGraph shall be consistent with the pin key. For example, U1.2
corresponds to pins.U1.pin_2.
4. The net key itself does not need to be consistent with the GT, but the pin set of the
net in hyperGraph shall be correct.

--- PAGE 35 ---
35
5. Line geometry must be placed under the edges of the correct net. Entries assigned
to a wrong net will not receive points merely due to geometric proximity.
6. Strings adopt strict exact- match comparison. 10k, 10K and 10 k are treated as
distinct strings.
7. Coordinates are in image pixels. The default threshold for both pins and lines
is 5 px.
